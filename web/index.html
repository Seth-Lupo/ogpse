Off-grid solar power for data centers in Pecos County, Texas

Seth LupoNovember 2026Code

A data center needs power day and night. Suppose you power one with only solar panels and batteries, with no grid. How much power can a piece of land give, and what does it cost?

I worked this out for each of the pieces of land over 2,000 acres in Pecos County, Texas. For each one I placed solar panels on the usable ground, ran 25 years of hourly weather through a solar model, and found the cheapest mix of panels and batteries that keeps the power on 99.9% of the time.

Results

Cost ranges from $M to $M per megawatt of steady power. The cheapest land is in the southwest. All of it together could supply about GW.

Yearly sunshine changes by only % across the county. Cost depends mostly on the worst stretches of cloudy days, because the system has to be big enough to get through them.

Land is about % of the cost. It sets how much power a site can hold, not the price per megawatt.

Bad weeks set the size

A simple guess is enough panels to cover a year's energy and enough battery for one night. At that is MW of panels and hours of battery per MW of load. Over 25 years of weather, it keeps the power on only % of the time.

Reaching 99.9% takes MW of panels and hours of battery, about times the cost. In the worst 5 days of the 25 years, the panels made only % of their usual power. The battery has to cover those days, and the extra panels refill it afterward.

Across West Texas, from El Paso to Abilene, yearly sunshine differs by %, but cost differs by %.

Method

  1. Land. Property lines from Texas GIO parcels (2026). Touching parcels with the same owners are joined into one piece of land. Parcels with the exact same outline are shared ownership of one piece, so they count once.
  2. Usable ground. Ground height every 10 m from USGS 3DEP. I remove ground too steep for solar trackers (over 8.5° north to south), wetlands (USFWS), and flood zones (FEMA).
  3. Panel rows. Rows run north to south, 9 m apart. Rows on ground that slopes too much end to end are skipped.
  4. Solar power. Hourly weather for 2000–2024 from NREL's NSRDB, run through pvlib for panels that turn to follow the sun.
  5. Sizing. I simulate the battery hour by hour. For each battery size, I search for the fewest panels that keep the power on 99.9% of the time, and keep the cheapest combination. Adding panels never lowers uptime, so a simple halving search finds the exact answer.
  6. Cost. Equipment prices from NREL's 2025 Annual Technology Baseline. Land at $1,000 per acre, which is my assumption.

Checks

  1. Panel layout. I tuned row spacing on 5 built solar plants in West Texas from the USPVDB, then predicted the size of 10 others. Average error was %, against % for a simple acres-per-MW rule. My goal was 15%. Real plants use 3.5 to 6.3 acres per MW, and the property outline doesn't show which.
  2. Solar model. NREL's SAM gives the same yearly energy within %.
  3. Uptime. I re-ran all final designs at full size. All keep the power on at least 99.9% of the time.

What's missing

Only building cost, not running costs, loans, or battery replacement. Hourly steps only, so nothing about faster electrical problems. No backup generators, which real campuses have and which would make the last 0.1% much cheaper. No soils, roads, easements, or oil and gas. Pecos County has no FEMA flood maps, so flood risk is unknown.

Every download is logged with its URL and checksum in data/manifest.csv. Owner names are only used to group parcels and are never shown.
