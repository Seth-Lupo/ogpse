Off-grid solar power for data centers in Pecos County, Texas

Seth LupoNovember 2026Code

A data center needs power around the clock. If you build one off the grid with only solar panels and batteries, how much power can a piece of land supply, and what does it cost?

I tried to answer that for every land holding over 2,000 acres in Pecos County, Texas, in all. For each one I laid out solar trackers on the land that is flat and dry enough, ran 25 years of hourly weather through a solar model, and found the cheapest mix of panels and batteries that keeps a constant load running 99.9% of the time.

Sizing for bad weeks

The simple way to size a system is enough solar to cover a year's energy and enough battery for one night. At that is MW of solar and hours of storage per MW of load. Simulated over 25 years, it keeps the load running only % of the time.

Getting to 99.9% takes MW of solar and hours of storage, about times the cost. The problem is cloudy stretches. In the worst 5 days of those 25 years, the panels made % of their usual output. The battery has to carry the load through that, and the extra panels have to refill it afterward.

This shows up across West Texas too. From El Paso to Abilene, annual solar output differs by %, but the cost of constant power differs by %.

Method

  1. Land. Parcels from the Texas state GIS office, merged into connected holdings with the same owners. Parcels with identical boundaries are shares of the same land, so they count once.
  2. Buildable area. A 10 m elevation grid from USGS lidar. I drop cells that are too steep for trackers (8.5° north–south) or inside a mapped wetland or flood zone.
  3. Layout. North–south tracker rows 9 m apart, skipping rows that would tilt too much end to end.
  4. Solar output. A pvlib model of single-axis trackers, run hourly on NSRDB satellite weather for 2000–2024.
  5. Sizing. For each battery size, I find the smallest solar array that keeps the load on 99.9% of the time in an hourly battery simulation, then keep the cheapest. A linear program would need a yes/no variable for each of the 219,000 hours. Bisection is simpler and exact here, because uptime only goes up as you add solar.
  6. Cost. Equipment prices from NREL's 2025 Annual Technology Baseline, and $1,000 per acre for land.

Checks

  1. Layout. I tuned row spacing on 5 built solar plants in West Texas, then predicted the capacity of 10 others. The average error was %, a little better than a simple acres-per-MW rule (%) but short of the 15% I was aiming for. Real plants use anywhere from 3.5 to 6.3 acres per MW, depending on design choices you can't see from the property line.
  2. Solar model. NREL's SAM, given the same inputs, agrees on annual energy within %.
  3. Uptime. I re-ran every final design at full scale. All reach 99.9%.

What's missing

It only counts construction cost, not operations, financing, or replacing batteries. It works hour by hour, so it says nothing about electrical stability. Real campuses keep backup generators, which would make the last 0.1% much cheaper; I left them out. It ignores soils, roads, easements, and oil and gas. And Pecos County has no FEMA flood maps, so flood risk is unknown.

Data: TxGIO StratMap parcels, USGS 3DEP, NREL NSRDB, FEMA NFHL, USFWS NWI, NREL ATB 2025, CEC equipment databases, USGS/LBNL USPVDB. Every download is logged with its URL and checksum in data/manifest.csv. Owner names are used only to group parcels and are not shown.
