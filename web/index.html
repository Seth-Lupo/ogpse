Off-grid solar power for data centers in Pecos County, Texas

Seth Lupo · November 2026 · Code

A data center needs power every hour of the year. If it is built off the grid with only solar panels and batteries, how much power can a given piece of land supply, and what will it cost?

This page answers that for each of the land holdings over 2,000 acres in Pecos County, Texas. For each holding it lays out solar trackers on the buildable land, simulates 25 years of hourly weather, and finds the cheapest mix of solar and batteries that keeps a constant load running in 99.9% of hours.

Why sizing from averages fails

The obvious way to size a system is to build enough solar to cover a year's energy and enough battery to get through one night. At that means MW of solar and hours of storage per MW of load. Simulated over 25 years, that system keeps the load running only % of the time.

Reaching 99.9% takes MW of solar and hours of storage, which costs times as much. The reason is runs of cloudy days. In the worst 5 days of the 25 years, the panels produced % of their normal output. The battery has to carry the load through that, and the extra panels have to recharge it afterward.

The same thing happens across West Texas. From El Paso to Abilene, annual solar output differs by %, but the cost of firm power differs by %.

Method

  1. Land. Parcels from the Texas state GIS office, merged into contiguous holdings with the same owners. Parcels with identical boundaries are shares of one piece of land, so they count once.
  2. Buildable area. A 10 m elevation grid from USGS lidar. Cells are excluded if they are too steep for trackers (8.5° north–south) or fall in a mapped wetland or flood zone.
  3. Layout. North–south tracker rows 9 m apart. A row is dropped if it would tilt too far end to end.
  4. Solar output. A pvlib model of single-axis trackers, run hourly on NSRDB satellite weather for 2000–2024.
  5. Sizing. For each battery size, find the smallest solar array that keeps the load on in 99.9% of hours, using an hourly battery simulation and bisection. Keep the cheapest combination. A linear program would need a yes/no variable for each of the 219,000 hours. Bisection works because uptime only rises as solar is added.
  6. Cost. Equipment from NREL's 2025 Annual Technology Baseline. Land at $1,000 per acre.

Checks

  1. Layout. After tuning row spacing on 5 built West Texas solar plants, the model predicted the capacity of 10 others within % on average. A simple acres-per-MW rule gets %. Built plants use 3.5 to 6.3 acres per MW depending on design choices that a property boundary can't show, so neither method does much better.
  2. Solar model. Compared with NREL's SAM on the same inputs over 25 years, annual energy differs by %.
  3. Uptime. Every design was re-simulated at full scale. All meet 99.9%.

Limits

Only construction cost is counted, not operations, financing, or battery replacement. The energy balance is hourly, so it says nothing about grid stability or faster effects. Real campuses keep backup generators, which would make the last 0.1% much cheaper. Soils, roads, easements, and oil and gas are not considered. Pecos County has no FEMA flood maps, so flood risk is unknown, not zero.

Data: TxGIO StratMap parcels, USGS 3DEP, NREL NSRDB, FEMA NFHL, USFWS NWI, NREL ATB 2025, CEC equipment databases, USGS/LBNL USPVDB. Every download is logged with its URL and checksum in data/manifest.csv. Owner names are used only to group parcels and are not shown.
